## The Archetypal Connection between Jung & Ibn 'Arabi Illuminated by Henry Corbin's Mundus Imaginalus

The bridge that connects Ibn 'Arabi's centuries-old mystical insights with Carl Jung’s 20th-century archetypal psychology centers on a radical proposition: the deepest interior truth of a human being can only be accessed through an inner Other, experienced symbolically as the feminine.

When Ibn 'Arabi states that woman is the mirror in which man contemplates his hidden being—the Self he must know to know his Lord—he is mapping a precise spiritual technology that Jung would later uncover through clinical dreamwork and active imagination.

1. The Mirror of the Other: Ibn 'Arabi's Theophanic Feminine

In Ibn 'Arabi’s metaphysics, God desired to see His own hidden perfection, so He brought forth creation as a cosmic mirror. At the micro-level, man does the exact same thing.

Man cannot look directly at his own essence without a medium, because the unreflected ego is blind to itself. By positioning woman as the supreme mirror, Ibn 'Arabi is not speaking merely of physical sociology; he is pointing to an ontological principle. The feminine represents Nature and the receptive, creative matrix (al-rahim) through which the Divine—and the human soul—takes form. 

To encounter the feminine in its highest spiritual reality is to encounter the inner face of one's own soul.

2. Jung's Anima: The Soul-Guide in the Mundus Imaginalis

Centuries later, Carl Jung independently arrived at this exact structure while mapping the architecture of the human psyche. Jung discovered that within the unconscious of a physical man resides the Anima—an autonomous archetypal image of the feminine that acts as an inner mediator.

The Bridge to the Unconscious: 

Just as Ibn 'Arabi described an intermediate spiritual realm (the mundus imaginalis or alam al-mithal) where non-physical realities take form, Jung found that dreams and active imagination operate in an autonomous psychological space.

The Psychopomp (Soul-Guide): 

In Jungian depth psychology, the Anima is not just a collection of personal memories about women; she is a psychopomp (a guide of souls). When a man embarks on the path of individuation—the journey to find his true, capital-S Self—he cannot get there through rational intellect or ego-will alone. He must plunge into the unconscious, and his guide through that labyrinth is the inner feminine image.

3. Active Imagination as the Meeting Ground

Both traditions agree that this journey cannot happen by sitting still and thinking abstractly. It requires dreamwork, and/or active imagination (or visionary engagement in the imaginal realm).

For Ibn 'Arabi and his commentator Henry Corbin, the mystic trains the heart to perceive the real, objective presences of the imaginal world, where the feminine figure acts as a theophanic witness, drawing the seeker out of his isolated ego into divine self-knowledge.

For Jung, the analysand practicing active imagination gives shape and voice to the figures bubbling up from the unconscious, allowing the Anima to speak, challenge, and ultimately lead the ego away from inflation and toward the center of the psyche—the true Self.

In both paradigms, man must pass through the mirror of the feminine to become whole. The journey to the true Self is fundamentally relational: the masculine ego must humble itself before the inner feminine soul-image, recognizing that what it seeks externally is actually the hidden depth of its own divine origin.

Of extreme importance is the understanding that: the Mundus Imaginalis is not imaginary or fantasy, but an intermediate realm-a bridge of shared objective meaning-that connects the everyday waking world of physical objects to the primordial, visionary world of the soul.  In both Ibn 'Arabi's metaphysics and Jung's Archetypal psychology, we learn that: within the physical man, the divine, contrasexual archetype of the inner woman or 'anima' represents the soul guide that leads the man to his Self-his archetype of completion or wholeness.

Re-reading this passage reminds me why French metaphysicist, Sorbonne professor, and Iranologist Henry Corbin (1903–1978) remains one of the most vital—yet criminally under-recognized—spiritual guides of the 20th century in the West.

While modern Western thought often reduces the imagination to mere fantasy or unreality, Corbin dedicated his life to recovering the Mundus Imaginalis (the Alam al-Mithal)—an objective, intermediate realm of real spiritual matter and archetypal forms where visionary events and theophanic experiences actually occur. For Corbin, the active imagination is not a psychological trick; it is the true organ of the soul, the mirror through which the divine reveals itself.

This insight wasn't limited to Ibn 'Arabi. Corbin was a master of Shiite angelology and the visionary traditions of esoteric Islam. Through his monumental work—such as his deep engagement with the 12th-century Persian mystic Suhrawardi and his translation and analysis of Avicenna and the Visionary Recitals—Corbin mapped out a universe alive with celestial lights, spiritual guides, and soul-journeys. He demonstrated how the great thinkers of the Iranian and Islamic worlds kept alive a philosophy of light, spiritual chivalry (javanmardi), and inner initiation that bridges Platonism with the prophetic traditions.

Tragically, despite influencing thinkers like Carl Jung and the Eranos circle, Corbin’s work is still far too little known outside of specialized academic and esoteric circles in the West. In a culture dominated by hyper-materialism and literalism, his retrieval of the "thought of the heart" and the sacred mirror of the self feels more urgent and revolutionary than ever.

Henry Corbin was a professor of Islamic Studies (and Islamic Philosophy) at the Sorbonne. He and Carl Jung had a profound mutual respect for each other. Corbin's Mundus Imaginalis, or Imaginal world describes the realm that Carl Jung was working within, when he descended into his collective unconscious, as recorded in his Red Book.
