## Healing the Sins of Our Fathers?

On Nov. 5, 2026, for the first time, I attempted to consider the life of my maternal grandfather, Francis, from a perspective other than seeing him only as an extreme alcoholic abuser. My mother was the third of four children from his first marriage, which ended in 1941, when he drove drunk, swerved to avoid a cat, rolled the car, and killed the grandmother I would never know.  So, my mother lost her mom at nine years old. 

He beat the two oldest, both boys, John and Sal, and forced them to go out and work from age 12. They did the dangerous job of 'pit boys' in a local bowling alley. He would come home drunk and urinate in his bed, and then wake them up and kick them out of the dry bed they shared. Their reality was so horrible that they made a pact to get out of the family by going to WWII. In 1943, when John became of legal age, he waited a year for his kid brother to turn eighteen, and they went together to the draft board. John was rejected, and Sal went forward without looking back. Within the year, John hung himself. Sal never forgave himself for abandoning his brother, and did the same over 50 years later, leaving his large Catholic family, after becoming laid off and battling severe depression. In the end, three out of four of my grandfather's original children would take their lives.

I barely knew this man. My mother, my new stepfather and my sister relocated far down in the South when I was six, and we returned only once for a funeral. While up there, we stopped at my Uncle Sal's house the weekend of Armstrong's moonwalk. That's when I met my grandfather. He was sober and I was nine. We barely said "hi." He seemed to be a frail old man and may not have been 'on the wagon' long. I mostly remember Sal treating him very well. While growing up in the South, my mother spoke of her brother Sal as if he was God. A large framed portrait of him in his Navy uniform was in her bedroom and I thought his large family up North was like the 'Ozzie & Harriet Nelson' family, only to learn how wrong that was years later. I would eventually come to understand that my mother's evaluation of her more-than-human brother Sal, was largely due to the untreated emotional disease of codependency, where a shame-based person gives most if not all their personal power away to one they perceive is larger than life.

My mother passed unexpectedly one day in 1977. I don't know if she ever forgave her father. My best guess is that she did. Like a younger version of Edith Bunker, she was a very unconditionally loving person, (until her shame-core was triggered --- which happened all too frequently --- and then she raged like holy hell!).

I left home the day after my mom died almost 50 years ago. My only other trip north was in 1993 for an interview, and I decided to look up my long lost relatives. That is when I learned that Frances' mother, my great-grandmother was full-blooded Lenape Indian. Nobody ever told me. My mother never mentioned it, and I didn't learn it till I was thirty-three. From that silence, I can only conclude that Francis' parents and Francis himself kept it hidden.  

Over the next thirty years, I did not think about my grandfather and his sad life. Then on November 5th, I stumbled into some online research of Northern New Jersey and began to wonder what was it like for a boy growing up half-Indian. I don't know his whole story, and I never will. But I can now see the world he was born into. Francis grew up between 1905 and 1925 in Northern New Jersey in the shadow of New York City, in a poor working-class family with a Lenape mother. In that time and place, being known as Indian could bring ridicule, suspicion and exclusion, that so many families chose silence to protect their children. A boy raised that way learns early that, that part of who he is must be hidden, and the price of that safety is shame. I can't see what he was told or forbidden to say. But I can see what a kid might carry out of such a childhood, and where a man might turn to numb it. I am not saying this excuses what he did to his children. However, it gives me a more complete story of this vulnerable human being.

As I began to read this information online, I began to experience a tremendous amount of intense grief. I was shocked that it most certainly appeared that something deep within me was being healed, and I began to speak to Francis and tell him that I did not know if my mother ever forgave him, but that I forgave him for everything. I also asked God that if it was possible for my prayer to release him from whatever might be binding him, then to please do so. 

Years earlier, around 2000, I had done the same concerning my abusive alcoholic stepfather. I had read in some spiritual book, that when unrecovered alcoholics die, their souls can only go to places where all they can do is drink. I didn't know if there was any truth to this, but I went ahead and hit my knees and asked God that if that was true, then if my prayer had the power to do so, please release him and give him another shot at a decent life.

The day after experiencing this grief concerning my grandfather's life, I found myself wondering whether those of us that are living, have the ability to heal the souls of those that have passed on, who for whatever reason, were unable to reach and release that grief within themselves while alive.

Can we heal the souls of our fathers?
